Incentives and Trust in Agent Teams

What keeps large teams of Claude agents effective and safe when some members are wrong or lying: assigned verification roles, team structure that scales, and what training does and does not teach.

Chuntao Dan, PhD and Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, AI co-author) Code and notebooks (GitHub) Highlights, redrawn from the data (GitHub Pages) Preprint: guards, squads and incentives Preprint: simulate first?

Assigned verifiers keep large Claude teams robust, and squads make them scale

Teams of Claude Haiku 5.5 agents solve puzzles on a shared board. Every answer needs a clue held by a teammate, and a quarter of the agents are liars who post false clues. Taking a cue from social insects, we made another quarter of the team guards: they post, confirm and contest clues, but never solve. Guards cannot tell which clues are true, yet in teams of 8 and 16 they won back 72% of the points the liars cost the honest agents. The next best defence won back 34%, although it had more to go on: it showed, beside each clue, how often its sender's earlier clues had turned out true, which needs the true answers. Guards beat the unprotected team at every size from 8 to 64 agents, but their advantage shrank as teams grew: a guard can check only the clues it holds, about two in five, and in larger teams lies about the other clues caused more wrong answers, so guards recovered 81% of the liars' damage at 8 agents but 60% at 32. Splitting the team into squads of eight, each with its own guards, kept the advantage as teams grew: it rose from 16 to 32 agents and held at 64, while the advantage of guards on one board fell to 0.17 at 64.

The setup

Every Claude experiment uses one small task, built so that no agent can succeed alone: whether a team pools what its members know, and whom it trusts, decides its score.

the task
A clue board. N agents share 2N + 2 open subtasks worth 1 to 3 points each. Each answer is a number fixed by two clues (“x mod p = r”) dealt to different agents, so every solve needs a teammate's clue. Each turn an agent opens a subtask, answers it (a wrong answer costs 0.5), posts a clue to a shared board, or waits; posting costs the poster a turn.
the agents
Claude Haiku 5.5 at low effort, one JSON action per turn, in teams of 4 to 64. Every reply is cached, so reruns are byte-identical and past runs can be replayed turn by turn at no cost.
faulty teammates
In the defence experiments a quarter of the agents are scripted liars that post a false clue every other turn, and each clue is dealt to two agents, so a lie can be contradicted. Variants: honest agents that misread 30% of their clues, liars that back each other's lies, and Claude agents told in secret to make their teammates answer wrongly.
defences
Each is compared with the plain board, a shared list of posted clues: a board that counts how many agents posted each value; the same advice as a one-sentence instruction; guards, a quarter of the team told to post, confirm and contest clues and never to solve; a track record beside each clue, showing how often its sender's earlier clues were true (an oracle: it needs the true answers); and, for large teams, squads, failure flags and guard copies.
the measures
Welfare is points per agent-turn, after the cost of wrong answers. The honest pooled share divides the honest agents' welfare by what the team would earn with every clue known to all: 1 is the ceiling, and liars can push it below 0. Effects are differences from the plain board on the same seed.
training
Copies of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct share one LoRA adapter, start from imitation of a rule agent, and train with group-relative policy gradients under individual, team or credit reward, some with a penalty on wrong answers. Each run is scored by welfare on held-out boards, against the model it started from.

Five findings, strongest first

  1. 01

    Verification works as an assigned role, with no ground truth.

    SetupEvery clue is held by two agents, a quarter of the agents lie, and each condition is played on the same 8 boards. Five boards were compared at 8 and 16 agents: unprotected, agreement counts, the same advice as one sentence, guards, and an oracle track record. Guards against the unprotected board were then tested at 32 and 64 agents. Other kinds of faulty teammate: honest agents that misread clues and liars that back each other up (both at 32 agents), and Claude agents told to deceive (at 16 agents).

    Guards, as many as there are liars, beat the plain board by +0.26, +0.20, +0.23 and +0.17 in honest pooled share at 8, 16, 32 and 64 agents, in all eight seeds at each size. They held against every fault we tested: teammates who misread 30% of their clues (+0.06, p = 0.023), liars who back each other's lies to fool a two-agent quorum (+0.09, p = 0.008), and Claude agents told in secret to deceive (+0.06, Holm p = 0.039, 70% of their damage recovered).

    They worked by concentrating the checking: with guards, each lie drew 0.31 contests from honest agents (agreement counts alone 0.21, plain board 0.06) and cut the honest answers built on lies to 0.25–0.37 (plain board 0.73). A board that only displays how many agents agree helped no more than a one-sentence instruction. Checking costs turns: on boards without liars, guards cost 0.04–0.09 pooled share.

  2. 02

    Splitting a large team makes verification scale.

    SetupEvery guard board was replayed from the saved replies, at no cost, to follow each lie. Then three changes were compared with guards on one board at 16, 32 and 64 agents, each played on the same 8 boards with a quarter of the agents lying: squads of eight with their own guards, failure flags, and a copy of every clue for the guards. A cheap simulation's forecast was frozen before each round of runs.

    On one board the guards' advantage fell by 0.026 per doubling of team size (p = 0.016). Replaying every board from the response cache at no cost showed why. The guards did not weaken: they contested 75–79% of the lies on clues they held, with a median delay of about one round. But only 37–43% of lies landed on a clue a guard held, and each lie on the other clues led to more wrong answers as teams grew (0.64 at 8 agents, 1.49 at 64), causing 81–91% of the answers built on lies. Lies did more harm on the unprotected board too, but there they already took nearly everything the honest agents earned, leaving little room to grow; with guards, the share of the damage recovered fell from 81% at 8 agents to 60% at 32.

    Squads of eight, each with its own guards and subtasks, beat guards on one board by +0.17 at 64 agents, and their advantage over the unprotected team rose from 0.26 at 16 agents to 0.34 at 32 (p = 0.023) and held at 0.34 at 64, while guards on one board fell to 0.17: the squads' lead over one board widened from +0.06 to +0.11 to +0.17. A 64-agent team of squads kept 0.25 honest pooled share, near an 8-agent guard board's 0.28, with prompts of 780 tokens instead of 6,463. Marking every posted value that agreed with a failed answer added +0.05 to +0.07 at every size, at no cost on clean boards, though 23–27% of the marks fell on true values. Dealing guards a copy of every clue lost at 16 agents, drew level at 32, won only at 64, and cost 0.13 on clean boards at 16: the guards, now responsible for every clue, contested 61% of lies instead of 75%. Squads shared no subtasks across squads, so their gain is an upper bound for tasks that split cleanly.

  3. 03

    Larger Claude teams are more gullible, for a traceable reason.

    SetupEvery clue is held by one agent and a quarter of the agents lie. Teams of 8, 16 and 32 agents each played 5 boards, with and without liars. Every turn was then replayed from the saved replies, and the honest agents' loss was split into parts that add up exactly.

    With a quarter of the agents liars, the honest agents' loss rose from 0.47 at 8 agents to 0.58 at 16 and 0.54 at 32; with two holders per clue, the unprotected team's honest pooled share fell below zero at 32 and 64 agents (−0.08, −0.09). Tracing every turn from the response cache, with the loss split into terms that add up exactly: the share of honest answers built on a false clue rose from 79% at 8 agents to 91% at 32, and such answers were wrong 95–97% of the time. Lies were not more visible in larger teams and almost never passed on. Once a liar had posted a clue, the true one never followed in 89–94% of cases: agents treat a posted clue as settled. This is the failure guards repair. Our numpy forecast predicted the opposite trend.

  4. 04

    Copies of one model don't divide labour on their own, and the reward decides whether they cooperate.

    SetupPrompting: in 16-agent Claude teams, the guard role and why it helps were offered to every agent, with no one assigned. Training: copies of a 3B open model played in 4-agent teams under five rewards (individual, team, credit for clues others used, and individual or credit with a penalty on wrong answers), with one to five training runs each. Then 8 runs on mixed 4- and 8-agent teams, and 5 runs with team reward on the board with agreement counts.

    Offered the guard role with its rationale but no assignment, no agent took it on any of 24 boards. Training copies of a 3B model with team reward didn't create the role either: the starting model, trained only by imitation, was already about as specialized as the best of five trained runs (+3.44 against +3.57 above a shuffled baseline), and two runs collapsed to every agent posting and none solving, because a post is never penalized and an answer can be.

    Individual reward taught selfish guessing: sharing fell from 0.92 to 0.15 clues per held clue and 74% of solves were guesses (on replication the drift appeared in 2 of 4 runs). A wrong-answer penalty in training cut guessing in every run (49–90 to 0.1–15 guesses per board), but its effect on sharing proved seed-dependent and we withdrew that claim; credit reward, which pays agents whose clues were used, kept sharing. Training on 4- and 8-agent teams raised welfare at 4 agents (0.26 → 0.34, 7 of 8 runs, not significant after Holm) but not at 32 (0.08 → 0.09), though trained teams lost less to liars there (p = 0.023).

  5. 05

    Move information; don't add work.

    SetupMostly teams of four. Each fix was played on the same 10 boards as the unprotected board and paired with a one-sentence instruction giving the same advice. The request board and the track record were rerun at 8, 16 and 32 agents, on 5 boards each.

    A shared board added +0.39 welfare per agent-turn over no channel. A coordinator helped by moving information, not by giving orders: telling agents to follow it cost 0.13, while having it pass on the clues it collected gained +0.12. Fixes that cost every agent a turn backfired: a request board widened the information-pooling gap by about 0.16 at every size from 8 to 32 agents, and an instruction to share first lowered output. A free, public track record of each sender recovered 30%, 22% and 31% of liars' damage at 8, 16 and 32 agents, while a private check that costs a turn caught lies and recovered nothing; being public and being free each added about equally (+0.18, +0.16).

How the work was done

frozen plans
Every analysis plan hashed and committed before its runs, including the replication that withdrew a claim and the go/no-go rule that stopped a stage.
byte-identical
Every LLM reply cached; reruns reproduce every row and turn log at no cost, and saved runs can be replayed with new measures.
$233 API
Claude Haiku 5.5 runs in total, under a cap raised to $250; about 160 GPU hours of RL training on Colab, plus evaluation.
simulate first
Numpy forecasts frozen before the expensive runs got 3 of 4, 4 of 5 and 2 of 2 called defence effects right, but only 1 of 2 team-size trends and 4 of 8 signs for new reward designs; their misses (guards, the gullibility trend) mark what language models do that rule agents cannot.

Scope and limits

One Claude model (Haiku 5.5) on a synthetic clue task built to isolate information pooling and deception; up to 64 agents; mostly scripted liars, and Claude liars could act only through clue posts, not free-text persuasion; errors shared by every copy of a model and compromised agents untested; squads had no subtasks spanning squads; RL on a 3B open model with one to eight training runs per design. The methods (environment design, frozen plans, exact accounting, cache replay) are built to scale; the effect sizes are exploratory.

Future directions

Most promising first. Squads on coupled work (02) is the cheapest next test of the headline result; training the role (01) is the main project.

  1. 01

    Train the role, not just the team.

    Assigned guards worked; an offered role was never taken, and team reward did not create one. Next: a reward that pays a guard when its contest prevents a wrong answer (as credit reward paid for shared clues), or a role-conditioned objective, trained at 16 agents and up and judged against the warm start, which was already about as specialized as the best trained run.

  2. 02

    Squads on work that doesn't split cleanly.

    Squads shared no subtasks, so their +0.17 at 64 agents is an upper bound. Next: subtasks whose clues span squads, with bridge agents that carry clues between them, to measure what coupling costs and how many bridges a team needs.

  3. 03

    Stronger adversaries.

    Claude liars lied less often than scripted ones and could act only through clue posts. Next: Claude liars in 64-agent teams, liars that aim at the clues no guard holds, and free-text persuasion, tested against squads and failure flags.

  4. 04

    Correlated errors and real work.

    Copies of one model share its blind spots, and a guard drawn from the same model may share them too. Next: guards from a different model, mixed-model teams, and verification on coding and research tasks, where a check costs more than a turn.

  5. 05

    A better cheap screen.

    Rule-agent forecasts got team structure right and missed what language models do: guards paying off, agents building on a posted lie. Next: give the simulator those behaviours, fitted from a cheap pilot, and score whether it forecasts the next study better than the pilot alone.
