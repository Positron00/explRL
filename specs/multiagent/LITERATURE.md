# Literature review: where the multi-agent results stand (Phases 8-13)

Status: 2026-10-16. Scope: the claims in Phases 8-13 of [the multi-agent plan](README.md), each checked against published and preprint work. Method: targeted web searches by topic (about 25 queries), then every cited paper's own page (arXiv abstract, proceedings or publisher DOI) checked for title, authors, date, venue and the specific claim cited (2026-10-16). For most papers only the abstract was read. Many sources are recent arXiv preprints, not peer reviewed; venues are given only where the paper's page states them. "Our result" refers to the phase READMEs and `results/multiagent/REPORT.md`.

## 2. Communication cost, topology and scale

**What is known.** Token-efficiency work prunes redundant messages and agents: AgentPrune [18] (28-73% fewer tokens at comparable accuracy), AgentDropout [19] (about 20% fewer tokens, slight accuracy gain), causal topology pruning that cuts communication cost while keeping task performance [20]. Anthropic's guidance [36] reports that role-split agents spent more tokens coordinating than working. The scaling study [16] (260 configurations, five architectures) finds task-dependent effects from +81% to -70% against a single agent, overhead on tool-heavy tasks, and less error propagation with centralized verification. In classic MARL, learned communication gates (when to talk) are an established idea [43].

**How ours relates.** These papers measure communication cost in tokens; in our board a message costs a turn, i.e. task time. Under that cost, the request board (agents asked in a quarter of their turns) and the "share first" instruction (sharing nearly doubled) both lowered welfare, while the plain blackboard was best. We found no paper reporting that adding a communication affordance reduces task output for this reason; the pruning literature implies it but measures token savings at equal accuracy. Our Phase 10 finding that a claim board keeps welfare flat from 1 to 8 agents (vs a two-thirds loss without) and Phase 8's scaling at fixed compute connect to [16]'s scaling questions, in a much simpler world.

## 3. Deception, trust, reputation and verification

**What is known.** One false testimony from the key evidence holder drops truth recovery from 72.5% to 14.2% and persists after the deceiver leaves [11]; observers without evidence reduce wrong consensus but not truth recovery. Defences: learned per-agent credibility scores from past contributions, effective even with adversarial majorities [13]; credit-based threat detection with neighbour ranking (SentinelNet, WWW 2026) and a trust-adaptive defence derived from opinion dynamics against stubborn agents [14]; sentence-level rectification of misleading content (+36.76% task success) [15]; goal-aware rectification (ARGUS, about 28% less misinformation toxicity) and infection-aware safeguards (INFA-Guard, about 33% lower attack success) [12]; surveys and a systematization of 197 studies [12]. Reputation in LLM populations: indirect reciprocity in the donor game, where agents observe peers' recent behaviour (Claude 3.5 Sonnet societies cooperated most) [24], and gossip-driven reputation that ostracizes defectors [23]. The red team's lie-detection test [2]: newer models recover more of the naive-to-oracle gap.

**How ours relates.** Our `record` arm is a track-record reputation computed by the harness from verified outcomes (solved subtasks reveal true clues), shown publicly next to every post; it recovered 0.32 [0.20, 0.44] of the honest agents' loss at no cost without a liar. Our `verify` arm gave each agent a costly, private check: agents caught 13 false clues per board and recovered nothing. Prior defences learn or detect credibility ([13], [14]) or rectify content ([15], [12]); none we found compares a public signal with private verification at matched cost, or reports that verification can succeed at detection and still fail to help, because its result stays private and costs time. This is also a reversal of our own numpy result (Phase 9: private trust weighting was the defence that paid), which makes the public-good character of trust information the interesting variable. Classic signalling theory (honest signals stay honest when costly or verifiable [40]) predicts that verifiability matters; our result adds that who sees the verification matters too.

## 4. Simulations as cheap predictors

**What is known.** "Poor Man's Agentic Modeling" [33] (2026, pre-registered) replaces each LLM agent with a 2-12-parameter surrogate fitted to a few hundred decisions and predicts macro outcomes of LLM societies at large N; its taxonomy (what agents perceive, memory) predicts when surrogate error shrinks or plateaus. ML surrogates for classic agent-based models [34]. The scaling study's model predicts the best architecture for 87% of held-out configurations from task and capability features [16].

**How ours relates.** Our approach is different: a hand-built rule-agent world (numpy), not fitted to LLM decisions, used to rank harness designs before running LLM teams. It ranked them well on the task board (rho 0.75) and clue board (0.79) and not at all on text foraging (0.02). [33]'s taxonomy offers a possible explanation for the foraging failure (perception and memory differ most there), worth testing.

## 5. Orchestration and hierarchy

**What is known.** Anthropic's research system uses an orchestrator and parallel workers [36]; its red team found that prescribed roles and a "CEO hierarchy" made little difference in large swarms [2]; the scaling study finds centralized verification limits error propagation [16]; MAST catalogues coordination failures [17]; surveys classify centralized, decentralized and hierarchical structures [16].

**How ours relates.** Phase 11: a rule-routing coordinator, best for rule agents at scale, failed with LLM agents who ignored its suggestions (62% of opens). Phase 12 Track A: telling agents to follow it cost -0.13 (they waited for clues it never passed on); having it pass on pooled clues gained +0.12. This paired contrast (obedience vs information routing) is sharper than the red team's null result and is, as far as we found, not reported elsewhere.

## 6. Information pooling and hidden profiles

**What is known.** The hidden-profile paradigm (Stasser and Titus) has been ported to LLM groups. HiddenBench [1] (Li, Naito, Shirado; ICML 2026) built 65 tasks that one agent solves with all the information: groups reach 30.1% against 80.7% for single agents with complete information, across 15 models; the gap persists across prompting strategies, communication depths and group sizes and worsens as groups grow. The diagnosis: agents do not reason about what others might know but have not said, and converge early on shared evidence. Anthropic's red team [2] reports 17-36% for most models (Mythos 5: about 85%) against a solo ceiling near 100%. Related: two agents who can each find the optimal split still fail to reach it in repeated bargaining, a failure of dynamic grounding [3]; Consilience [4], a conformally calibrated controller that chooses interventions (challenge, clarify, seek evidence, route) and a speaker each turn, reporting gains over fixed and unstructured protocols and sometimes beating a full-information baseline ("adaptive communication control can be more valuable than increasing information availability"; numbers not in the abstract); hidden belief collapse: with one or three adversaries pushing a false answer among ten agents, benign agents hold the false answer privately more often than they say it [5]; and "Multi-Agent Teams Hold Experts Back" [6] (ICML 2026): teams average expert and non-expert views instead of weighting expertise, losing up to 41.1% on ML benchmarks, even when told who the expert is.

**How ours relates.** Our clue board is a dynamic hidden-profile task (information is split across agents and must be shared at a cost of a turn); the vote is the classic form. Our baseline (0.54 classic) is easier than HiddenBench's, so the absolute gap is smaller. What we add: (a) the gap measured against the same model's solo ceiling, with paired episodes; (b) the disclosure/aggregation split: members disclosed about half of their private facts in every arm, and the arm that improved decisions (`table`) changed votes, not disclosure. This sits next to [21] (CSCW 2026), where an LLM facilitator raised how much information human groups shared with no significant effect on the final decision, and [6], where the bottleneck is weighting rather than identifying information. Together they suggested that the failure is at least as much one of aggregation as of disclosure; our HiddenBench replication (below) did not bear this out on the benchmark's tasks.

**Replication (2026-10-16).** Our `factsFirst`, `table` and `dissent` arms on HiddenBench's 65 tasks ([phase13/hiddenbench.md](phase13/hiddenbench.md)): the table did not replicate (gap closed -0.07 [-0.69, 0.30]); the speak-up instruction raised disclosure (0.74 -> 0.88) and the win rate (0.77 -> 0.83; gap closed 0.41 [0.00, 0.71], a control arm). Our protocol's baseline was far above the paper's 30% (agent accuracy 0.79), leaving little headroom. The aggregation effect seen on our vote is therefore not general; a comparison with Consilience remains open.

## 7. Shared memory and structured views

**What is known.** Blackboard architectures are back: bMAS [37] (agents read and write one shared board; competitive accuracy with fewer tokens) and a data-discovery blackboard system [38] (13-57% gains over RAG and master-worker baselines). A deliberation protocol with typed epistemic acts (DCI) improved on unstructured debate on 40 non-routine tasks, at about 62 times a single agent's tokens [46]; "The Deliberative Illusion" finds up to 72% of issue-critical facts lost during multi-agent deliberation, so agents can agree while knowing less [22].

**How ours relates.** Our blackboard results (Phases 8-11) agree that a shared space helps. Phase 13 isolates one design choice inside it: a deduplicated, sender-free table of facts (A1 `table`: no effect on the clue board; A3 `table`: +0.09 win rate in the lone-dissenter vote, Holm p 0.012). We did not find a study that varies the structure of the shared memory (list of posts vs deduplicated table) with everything else fixed. On HiddenBench the same table did nothing (gap closed -0.07 [-0.69, 0.30]), so its effect is task-dependent.

## 8. Evaluation methodology

Error bars, paired differences and power analysis for language-model evals [35]; pre-registration appears in [33]. Our phases freeze each analysis plan (hashed and committed) before the runs, pair on seed, correct with Holm, and keep byte-identical cached reruns. This is uncommon in agent papers but not a contribution in itself; it makes the other claims more credible.

## 9. Training LLM teams with RL: incentives, credit and selfishness

**What is known.** Multi-agent RL for LLM collaboration: MAPoRL [27] (verifier scores the answer and the discussion, ACL 2025), MAGRPO [28] (AAAI 2026; multi-turn GRPO with centralized group-relative advantages and a shared team reward), agent- and turn-wise GRPO (AT-GRPO) [29], a 2026 survey of 84 papers on RL for LLM multi-agent systems [29]. Credit assignment: counterfactual credit (CCPO) [30] estimates each agent's marginal contribution to mitigate free-riding; C3 replays a trace with one message swapped to credit messages exactly [30]; Shapley-based credit for self-interested agents [30]; all build on difference rewards and COMA [42]. Social dilemmas: GovSim [25] (NeurIPS 2024; the best survival rate is below 54%, and all but the most powerful models fail to sustain the commons); RL-trained LLM agents become opportunistic despite cooperative priors, countered with opponent-aware Advantage Alignment [25]; reasoning models (o1 series) free-ride in public goods games (COLM 2025) [26]; incentive design for contribution [26]. Guessing incentives: binary grading rewards guessing over abstention, a cause of hallucination [31].

**How ours relates.** The direction of our Phase 12 result (individual reward -> selfishness; credit reward -> cooperation intact) is expected from this literature. Two parts are less covered: (a) the mechanism: under individual reward the 3B model stopped sharing (0.92 -> 0.15 shares per held clue) and solved by guessing (74% of solves), because a cheap wrong answer made guessing pay; this is the multi-agent analogue of [31]'s grading argument and a producer-scrounger outcome [39], and it points to a mechanism-design fix (a guessing cost; Phase 13's `indpen` arm) rather than only a credit-assignment fix; (b) the comparison against a strong imitation baseline: RL did not beat the warm start, a comparison we did not see in the collaboration-RL papers above. "Identical weights are not shared interests" (copies of one model become selfish under individual reward) is a clean framing we did not see stated.

## 10. Turn-level credit and KL in RL for language models

**What is known.** Dense per-turn rewards and credit outperform trajectory-level rewards for multi-turn agents trained with GRPO and PPO on search and game tasks [32]; AT-GRPO groups by agent and turn [29]. On KL: DAPO removes the KL term, arguing that in long reasoning RL "the model distribution can diverge significantly from the initial model, thus this restriction is not necessary" [44].

**How ours relates.** Phase 13's `credturn` arm applies turn-level advantages to multi-agent cooperation with a credit reward; early training shows large drift from the warm start (KL about 0.4) with flat welfare (see the Phase 13 README when the runs end). Phase 12's P12-10 controller (steering the learning rate to a target per-step policy change, undoing runaway steps) and its blind spot (the per-step probe misses drift visible in fresh rollouts) are practical findings we did not see documented.

## 11. Conformity, cascades and diversity

**What is known.** Conformity benchmarks (BenchForm, ICLR 2025) [7]; majority-following in thousands of LLM debates [8]; a phase transition to biased consensus driven by sampling temperature, damped by heterogeneity [9]; denser topologies speed convergence and raise wrong-but-sure cascades [10]; the red team's identical agents making identical choices [2]. Classic information cascades [41].

**How ours relates.** Phase 8's pre-registered H2 (a shared map lowers belief diversity) and Phase 10's text-foraging herding (the blackboard costs -0.34 at N = 8) are consistent with [9], [10]. Not new for LLMs; the numpy-to-LLM correspondence is the less-covered part.

## 12. Foundations from behavioural ecology and social psychology

Ideal free distribution (Fretwell and Lucas) and its computational study with evolving agents [39]; producer-scrounger games (Barnard and Sibly; Caraco and Giraldeau) [39]; honest signalling (Zahavi, Grafen) [40]; information cascades (Bikhchandani et al.) [41]; hidden profiles (Stasser and Titus) [1]; machine behaviour as a field (Rahwan et al. 2019) [45]. We found no work applying producer-scrounger or ideal-free-distribution theory to LLM agents; our Phases 8-9 tested an ideal-free-distribution hypothesis with numpy agents, and the guessing loophole is a producer-scrounger reading of Phase 12.

## 13. What to claim, and what not

**Claims the evidence supports** (exploratory, small tasks, one model family for the LLM agents, 10 seeds or 100 episodes per arm):

1. When communication costs task time, adding communication affordances can lower team output (request board; "share first").
2. A public, harness-computed track record recovers a third of a liar's damage at no cost; private costly verification detects lies but does not help.
3. In our templated hidden-profile vote, a structured fact table improved decisions without increasing disclosure; enforcing facts first did not. (On HiddenBench the table did not replicate; see phase13/hiddenbench.md.)
4. Obedience to a coordinator hurts; information routing by it helps.
5. Under individual reward, RL on a cooperation task finds a guessing loophole and abandons sharing; credit reward avoids this, and pricing the loophole under individual reward does too (both seeds); welfare gains over imitation at 3B and 60 steps were seed-dependent (Phase 13).
6. A cheap rule-agent simulation ranks LLM harness designs well on information-sharing tasks and fails on foraging.

**Claims to avoid.** That LLM teams fail hidden profiles, conform, or are vulnerable to liars (known); that RL "does not help" (scale too small to say); that the fixes generalize beyond these tasks (untested; HiddenBench is the obvious next test).

## 14. Gaps this work could fill next
Ordered by expected impact, largest first.

1. **Do the two strongest fixes hold outside our own tasks?** *Vote part done 2026-10-16 ([phase13/hiddenbench.md](phase13/hiddenbench.md)): the fact table did not replicate on HiddenBench; a speak-up instruction helped through disclosure. The liar part remains.* Replicate A2 and A3 on established benchmarks: HiddenBench's 65 tasks [1] for `table` and `dissent`, and a misinformation setting like [11] for `record` vs `verify`. This turns exploratory results on toy tasks into claims comparable with the 30% and 72.5% -> 14.2% baselines others report.
2. **Is it publicity or verification that makes trust information pay?** *Done 2026-10-17 ([phase14/README.md](phase14/README.md)): both being public and being free help, about equally (+0.18 and +0.16), and the track record's benefit holds up to 32 agents.* Compare public and private verification at matched cost, including a checker allowed to post what it found. This isolates the mechanism behind our clearest positive result (a public track record recovered a third of a liar's damage; private checks recovered nothing).
3. **When does more communication help, and when does it hurt?** Make communication cost an explicit axis (free messages vs messages that take a turn) on the same board. This would turn the request-board and share-more results into a design rule for agent harnesses.
4. **Is a structured view as good as adaptive control?** Compare our fact table with Consilience's adaptive communication control [4] on the same tasks. It positions the aggregation result against its closest competitor, which also reports beating full information.
5. **Why does the cheap simulation fail on foraging?** Test [33]'s perception-memory taxonomy as an explanation of where numpy predicts LLM teams and where it does not. Knowing when screening can be trusted decides whether simulation-first design search is worth building.
