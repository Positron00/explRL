## Main conclusions in plain language

The project rebuilt computer models of how a fruit fly finds food, then asked how many such agents searching together should share what they know: not at all, on a shared noticeboard, by private messages, or through a coordinator. Later, Claude language models replaced the simple agents.

1. **Sharing helps, and a shared noticeboard helps most:** teams that pool what they find spread over food sources better and recover faster when the food moves (Phase 8).
2. **Sharing makes everyone think alike:** efficient when the information is right, dangerous when it is wrong (Phase 8).
3. **One liar does the most damage where trust is highest:** a false post hurts the noticeboard team more than the messaging team; the only defence that paid was each agent privately tracking whom to trust (Phase 9).
4. **Bigger teams are not automatically better per member:** each extra agent adds less; the team total grows only with coordination (Phases 8, 10).
5. **Cheap simulations predict AI teams on most tasks** (rank agreement about 0.75-0.8), so team designs can be tested nearly for free first (Phases 10, 11).
6. **When no one can solve a task alone, AI teams depend on sharing:** with clues split across agents, the noticeboard roughly tripled earnings over no sharing; messages faded as teams grew (Phase 11).
7. **A coordinator helps by moving information, not by giving orders:** best for rule-following agents, it failed with AI agents that mostly ignored it (Phase 11). Telling them to follow it made things worse (they then waited for clues it never passed on); having it pass on the clues it collected helped (Phase 12).
8. **Wording and model size changed little:** "you are scored as a team" did nothing clear; at matched effort the stronger model gained as much from sharing as the smaller one; at an equal budget two agents beat one, but more did not pay (Phase 10, follow-ups).
9. **Ordinary puzzles were too easy to need teamwork:** teamwork became essential only when information, not difficulty, was the bottleneck (Phases 10, 11).
10. **Imitation teaches cooperation; RL incentives shape it, less reliably than a few seeds suggest:** a 3B open model learned the board by copying rule agents (half of Haiku's score); paying each agent for its own solves made it guess in every run and stop sharing in some; pricing that guessing loophole cut guessing in every run, and kept two seeds sharing, but with four and five runs per arm the sharing effect was seed-dependent (no test survived Holm), and welfare gains over imitation were not established (Phases 12-13, 16; revised 2026-10-18).

Bottom line: how a team shares information matters as much as how capable its members are. A shared space works well but spreads errors and lies fastest; structures that rely on obedience break with independent agents. Training an open model to cooperate (Phase 12) worked through imitation, not RL: how agents are rewarded decides whether they cooperate, and credit for enabling others' work is the reward that keeps them cooperating.

## Why

The goal is a portfolio piece for a multi-agent research role (Anthropic, Staff+ Research Scientist, Multi-Agent). The role asks for: training environments and data, comparisons of agent harnesses (memory, context management, communication), rigorous benchmarks for large agent teams, and attention to incentives, mechanism design and safety.

explRL is small and numpy-based, so it cannot show scale. It can show design and rigor, and a final phase repeats the key experiment with real language-model agents.

**Aim for one clear, well-measured finding, not many shallow modules.**

## Headline question

> At a fixed total compute budget, how does team performance change with team size under different communication harnesses, and how robust is each harness when one agent lies?

The paper suggests a hypothesis. Its flies learn fast because they keep a stable internal map and move only a goal. The team version is a **shared goal map** (a blackboard that every agent reads and writes). Hypothesis:

> Learned agents approach the ideal free distribution faster with a shared map, at the cost of less belief diversity and more vulnerability to liars.

Each of its three parts is a test fixed before running (Phase 8). Whatever the result, it gets reported with confidence intervals.

## Ideas from ecology and sociology

Used to **predict, measure and test** behaviour, not as built-in agent rules (Bitter Lesson). The interesting result is whether learned agents rediscover them, and under which harness. Animal and human results come from different constraints, so they are hypotheses, not claims about LLM agents.

| Idea | Source | Role in the plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideal free distribution**: selfish foragers spread over patches until every patch pays the same per forager | Fretwell & Lucas 1970 | Phase 6 reference: where selfish agents should end up, computed exactly, so the benchmark can say how far a team is from it |
| **Stigmergy with evaporation**: ants coordinate by marking the environment, and old marks fade | ant pheromone trails (e.g. Bonabeau, Dorigo & Theraulaz 1999) | Phase 7: the blackboard is stigmergy; an evaporation rate is a memory setting |
| **Social influence reduces crowd wisdom**: sharing estimates cuts diversity and makes groups confidently wrong | Lorenz et al. 2011 (PNAS) | Phase 8 metric: belief diversity and herding |
| **Commons governance**: monitoring and graduated sanctions keep shared resources healthy | Ostrom 1990 | Phase 9 defence against liars and free-riders |

## Phase 5: shared interface (foundation)

Full spec: [phase5/](phase5/README.md). **Status: implemented (2026-10-11).** Checked against the pre-Phase-5 commit: golden fixtures and all six demo `metrics.json` hashes identical; full flexible demo 4.6% slower.

- **API (PettingZoo "agent-environment cycle" style):** agents act one at a time, which matches the event-based agents. `env.reset(seed)`; `env.next_agent()` returns the agent whose next event is earliest; `env.observe(agent)`; `env.step(action)` applies that agent's action and returns its reward and whether it is done. A single agent is a team of size 1.
- **Time:** the existing agents are event-based, with variable fixation lengths and 0.32 s saccades. An **event queue** holds each agent's next event time; the env always processes the earliest. With one agent this reduces exactly to the current loops, provided random numbers are drawn in the same order (a design constraint for the spec).
- **Adapters** wrap `flexibleAgent`, the prestructured agent and `circuitAgent` behind one `event(world, t)` method that holds the old loop body unchanged (the circuit agent learns in the middle of a saccade, so a strict act-then-learn split would change its results). New agents write only `act` and `learn`; a default `event` calls them. `HeadingArena` and `ZoneArena` stay unchanged: the adapters call their methods directly, as the old loops do. New worlds (Phase 6 on) implement a small protocol: `reset(seed, nAgents)`, `observe(i)`, `apply(i, action)` (Phase 6 adds the start time: `apply(i, action, t)`). One training loop replaces the three separate ones (the old ones stay as thin wrappers).
- **Time units:** never converted. The flexible agent keeps counting integer 1 ms samples; the other agents use seconds; all members of one team must share a unit.
- **Unified history:** one record per event (`agent`, `t`, `dt`, `kind`, `location`, `reward`, extras such as the compass heading or goal), exportable as JSONL. `location` is where the reward was earned, at the end of the event (on the ring: the heading in radians, or the grid index for the circuit agent). These logs double as training data.
- **Acceptance:** all three existing demos give **byte-identical** `metrics.json` through the new loop, and short seeded runs saved from the old code ("golden fixtures") match exactly. Runs are already deterministic, so this is a strict regression check. The full flexible demo may be at most 25% slower (the others at most 10%). All tests pass.

## Phase 6: team environment with incentives

Full spec: [phase6/](phase6/README.md). **Status: implemented (2026-10-11).** Run guide: [phase6/RUN.md](phase6/RUN.md). Demo: [`nbs/demo_team.ipynb`](../../nbs/demo_team.ipynb). Result: the T1 prototype passed with the default settings for both layouts and both patch models (main: ring, fixed intake), and the demo's re-run of checks (1) and (2) on the package world passed too. The spec also settles how an event-based world pays harvest (claimed at the start of a stay), passes the start time to `apply`, and adds a reward hook to `CircuitMember` (decisions S1-S9).

- **The world is a patch graph, not a fixed dimension.** It is a set of locations, a **travel-time matrix** between them and a **communication range**. Two layouts generate the matrix:
  - **Ring**: the native heading ring with sectors. Used for the paper's agents and the bridge from Phase 5.
  - **2-D torus**: a grid whose edges wrap around. Search area grows with the square of its size, travel time grows with distance, and the communication range defines neighbourhoods from position. Expected to be the main layout for team sizes up to 64; the T1 prototype instead chose the ring (both layouts passed, the ring more clearly), so the torus is a configuration.
  3-D is out of scope: it adds cost but nothing the coordination, communication and incentive questions need.
- **Actions:** generic agents choose "stay" or "go to location j", travel directly, are charged the travel time and see nothing on the way. On the ring, the paper's agents keep their native actions (fixations and saccades with their own timing); the world maps their heading to a location.
- **`TeamPatchWorld`:** N agents in one layout. Some locations are resource patches, either with a fixed intake rate or with a stock that regrows (logistic, so it can be over-harvested); the prototype picked fixed intake as the default. Agents in the same patch split the harvest, so crowding hurts. Danger locations cost reward. Patches can move every T seconds (non-stationary), which makes exploring and sharing information worth something.
- **Interfaces:** `TeamPatchWorld` implements the Phase 5 world protocol (`reset`, `observe`, `apply`, with `apply` now also given the event start time) for generic agents, uses seconds as its time unit, and on the ring also offers the native methods the circuit agent's adapter calls (`ringActivity`, `valence`, and `fixate` for crowding).
- **Observations:** an agent sees its own location, the travel times from there (it knows the layout, not where patches are) and its own (optionally noisy) harvest. It learns about other locations only by going there, or through the Phase 7 channel.
- **Difficulty settings (work in any layout):** number of patches, patch richness (intake, or stock size and growth), how often patches move, observation noise, travel-cost scale, communication range.
- **Reward schemes:** individual; team (mean of all); **difference reward** (team harvest minus the team harvest if this agent were absent). Team and difference rewards are computed by the world from its yield model at the current crowding, because an event-based world has no common "step"; welfare always uses the harvest actually taken.
- **Agents:** the circuit agent (paper-inspired, built-in structure; **ring only**, because its goal weights encode a direction, not a place); **`banditAgent`** (a generic UCB learner over locations, with values discounted by travel time; both layouts); **`randomAgent`** (moves at random; the floor). Circuit vs bandit is a Bitter Lesson comparison: built-in structure vs a general learner, as team size and compute grow.
- **Two references, computed numerically for each world (from the patches' yield model, so they do not depend on the layout):**
  - **Ideal free distribution** (where selfish agents should settle): every occupied patch pays the same per agent. With equal sharing and fixed patch intake, this puts agents in proportion to each patch's intake (the "input matching rule").
  - **Social optimum** (the most total harvest any allocation can get). In the simple case it coincides with the ideal free distribution. With stocks that deplete and regrow, selfish agents over-harvest and the two differ: this gap is where reward schemes matter.
  Travel costs are left out of both, so the optimum is an upper bound on welfare.
- **Metrics:** welfare (total harvest; from Phase 7 on, harvest minus the danger cost, so time in danger lowers welfare), fairness (Gini), coverage of patches, time spent in danger, **distance from the ideal free distribution** over time (and time to get within a set distance after patches move), and the welfare lost relative to the social optimum.
- **Prototype first (decides the main layout).** Task T1 of the Phase 6 spec, before any package code: a throwaway numpy script with simplified versions of the Phase 7 channels (none / blackboard / messages), ring vs 2-D torus, team sizes 1, 4, 16, 64, about 10 seeds, `banditAgent`, both patch models. **Pass check ("dynamic range"):** (1) no ceiling: no harness gets within 10% of the social optimum (for that team size) in the first quarter of a run; (2) no floor: the no-communication team beats `randomAgent` at every team size (95% confidence intervals do not overlap); (3) the gap between the best and worst harness is significant (its 95% confidence interval, paired over seeds, excludes 0) for at least two team sizes and changes with team size. The prototype steps agents together each second, so the Phase 6 demo re-runs checks (1) and (2) on the real event-based world. The layout that passes most clearly becomes the main one; the other stays as a configuration. If neither passes, adjust the difficulty settings and repeat before going on to T2.
- **Sanity acceptance:** on the ring, configured like `ZoneArena` (two fixed safe and two danger zones, constant reward, no depletion), one circuit agent gives a history identical to `ZoneArena` through the Phase 5 loop (same times, rewards and native fields; the patch-aware adapter only adds a harvest field). Everything else is a reported finding, not an assert.
- **Scene symmetry for circuit agents (`ZoneArena` or the ring patch world):** keep **symmetric** scenes when studying compass remapping (Fig 4c, high jump rates) or when matching paper demos and Phase 5 regression fixtures. Prefer **asymmetric** scenes for new team experiments where the story is goal learning, PI, and coordination without constant 180° jumps. Always record the scene as `scene_symmetric` in `metrics.json` (as `demo_multi` does).

## Phase 7: harness configurations

Full spec: [phase7/](phase7/README.md). **Status: implemented (2026-10-11).** Channels (`none`, `blackboard`, `messages`, `hierarchical`), `CommBandit` / `LeadFollower` / `CircuitCommMember`, `runHarness`, `rangeCheck` and `demo_harness` are in the package; run guide: [phase7/RUN.md](phase7/RUN.md). Decisions H1-H10 settle where the channel lives (outside the world), how reports are read, message timing, the lead (a coordinator that does not forage) and the budget. The benchmark world (`benchWorld()`) differs from the Phase 6 default: a 64-location ring with 16 patches (total intake 32, so the social optimum keeps rising up to 64 agents), travel scale 0.48, patches moving every 40 s and harvest noise. **Harness check (AC-11): passes** (third attempt; the first two saturated because 6 agents could reach the optimum; larger maps also pass but leave small teams near zero, see the Phase 7 attempt log), so Phase 8 can start. Early findings: the blackboard is the best channel from 4 agents up; `messages` adds nothing over `none` at the defaults (one report per 10 s against the agent's own full history); the lead does worse than independent bandits at most sizes.

- **Communication channels:** `none`; `blackboard` (a shared value map per location); `messages` (each agent may send k short messages per period, each a location and a value); `hierarchical` (a lead collects reports and assigns agents to the best reported patches, taking crowding into account; a simple greedy rule, labelled as a baseline. The Phase 7 spec makes the lead a coordinator that does not forage and uses no agent-seconds, decision H7).
- **Reading the channel:** `banditAgent` treats reports as extra observations with a weight (1 = as good as its own). For the circuit agent, a report acts as an extra reward-gated goal update toward the reported heading. Details in the Phase 7 spec (Q8).
- **Communication range:** messages reach only agents within the range (unlimited = everyone). On the 2-D torus this makes the communication network follow from position, so its effect on exploration (Lazer & Friedman 2007) can be measured rather than imposed.
- **Memory:** none, a window of the last K events, or full history, counted per location (as Phase 6 decided for `banditAgent`; "none" keeps only the latest entry, K = 1; Phase 7 decision H5). For `banditAgent` this is a sliding-window estimate; for the shared channel it limits which entries are kept; for LLM agents (Phase 10) it is context management. The circuit agent's memory is its weights, so this setting does not apply to it.
- **Evaporation (stigmergy):** blackboard entries fade at a set rate (0 = never). Fast fading forgets stale patches after they move; slow fading keeps more information but also keeps outdated or false entries longer.
- **Two compute budgets, both reported:**
  - **Fixed compute** (headline): total agent-seconds (N x T) is held constant, so larger teams run for less world time each. This measures parallel vs serial use of the same compute (the "groups of agents as test-time compute" axis). Larger teams also spend a larger share of their run learning from scratch; that is part of the trade-off, not a flaw.
  - **Fixed world time:** every team runs for the same T, so compute grows with N. This shows the benefit of simply adding agents.
- Team sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.

## Phase 8: benchmark and eval suite

Full spec: [phase8/](phase8/README.md). **Status: implemented (2026-10-11).** Run guide: [phase8/RUN.md](phase8/RUN.md). **Result:** on 30 unseen seeds, run once against the frozen analysis plan, all three confirmatory tests are supported (Holm p = 0.0003 each): at fixed compute the blackboard raises welfare per agent-second (+0.091 [0.088, 0.093], averaged over 2-64 agents), recovers the ideal free distribution faster after patches move (-7.5 s) and lowers belief diversity (-0.109). Exploratory: independent bandits collapse at fixed compute as teams grow (0.48 at 4 agents to 0.07 at 64) while the blackboard holds (0.27 at 64); with 64 agents sharing a board the team optimum is more exploration than the default; in study B, learning the exploration level across episodes with discovery credit closes 45% of that gap, without credit it does worse than the default, and persisting stale maps hurts. The spec fixes the confirmatory analysis before running (decisions B1-B13): confirmatory seeds 100-129, never used before; three tests (P: blackboard vs none welfare at fixed compute; H1: recovery time; H2: belief diversity), sign-flip permutation tests with Holm. It also replaces the IFD distance with a tie-invariant one (the benchmark world's equal-intake patches made the old one overstate distances, up to 0.90 instead of 0.64 at N = 4), redefines H1 as a censored restricted mean recovery time, runs seeds in parallel (main grid about 31 CPU-minutes), and turns the group-vs-individual and persistence ideas below into studies A and B.

- **Main grid (fixed before running):** main layout, `banditAgent`, the 4 channels, 7 team sizes, both budgets, the main reward scheme (Q9), 30 seeds. Output is a tidy CSV, figures and an auto-written markdown report under `results/multiagent/`.
- **Side sweeps, one factor at a time** from the main grid's settings: reward scheme, evaporation rate, communication range, memory, layout, agent type (circuit agent on the ring). This keeps the run count manageable instead of crossing every factor.
- **Primary metric:** welfare (harvest minus danger cost) per agent-second at fixed compute, in the non-stationary world.
- **Group vs individual reward (planned comparison):** does rewarding the group let some agents sacrifice their own harvest to explore and share, raising the team total? A Phase 7 pilot (bandits, 5 seeds, 600 s, net welfare per agent-second) shows the scheme alone is not enough: with the **team** reward (everyone gets the group mean) bandits cannot tell which location earned their reward, so they stop learning where to go and spend more time in danger (N = 16 with the blackboard: 0.04 vs 0.47 individual; negative at N = 64); the **difference** reward (each agent's marginal contribution) matches or slightly beats individual (0.49 vs 0.47). Neither makes a bandit explore *for others*, because its rule only maximises its own estimate.
  - **The dilemma exists (pilot, 5 seeds):** with a blackboard and 64 agents the team does best when every agent explores more than the default (UCB bonus `cUCB` 2.0: 0.315 per agent-second vs 0.277 at 1.0), while without sharing less exploration is best (`none`, N = 64: 0.273 at 0.5 vs 0.203 at 1.0). Agents that pick their own level without credit settle near 1.0. Sharing turns exploration into a public good at large team sizes.
  - **Discovery credit** (`explRL/credit.py`, `nbs/credit.ipynb`): the first agent to harvest at a patch since it moved is its discoverer and is paid `beta` x every later harvest others take there, as a learning signal (not welfare). A `ScoutBandit` learns, by UCB over two modes with a warm-up, whether to exploit or scout (go to its least-known location not believed bad); credit goes to the mode that made the discovery. **Result (5 seeds, blackboard):** credit shifts behaviour the intended way (scout share at N = 64: 0.13 at `beta` 0, 0.16 at 1, 0.20 with the difference reward), but every scouting variant lowers welfare (N = 64: −0.02 to −0.05 per agent-second; worse on 128 and 256 location maps), because the scout action ignores travel cost and 91% of discoveries are made while exploiting. A scratch prototype in which credit tunes each agent's own `cUCB` level instead also moved exploration up (mean `cUCB` 1.0 to 1.3) without raising welfare; switching level every event and the warm-up cost more than the gain.
  - **Design used in Phase 8 (study B; results in the Phase 8 status above):** credit tunes a *persistent* exploration level per agent (fixed within a run, learned across runs), compared with the team-optimal fixed level (found by a `cUCB` sweep, and by fixed-level arms on the same worlds); measures: welfare gap closed, the level each agent uses (mean, spread and stability across episodes), free-riding index. The Phase 8 spec makes this study B (decision B11) together with the persistence side study below, and the reward-scheme and `cUCB` comparisons study A (B10).
- **Side study: persisting learned harness state across runs (exploratory).** Can a team improve with experience when its world changes? Each team plays a sequence of episodes (12 x 600 s in the Phase 8 spec), each on a new world seed, so patch sites differ every episode. Three arms, paired over seeds: (a) **fresh** — every agent starts each episode from scratch (current behaviour); (b) **strategy persists** — each agent keeps its strategy-level state: its exploration level values (the credit-tuned `cUCB` above; the report weight `w` is fixed, so there is nothing of it to persist until Phase 9 learns trust); (c) **strategy and map persist** — also keeps its per-location value estimates and the blackboard, which go stale when sites change (expected to hurt: a negative control for stale memory). Crossed with credit on and off (`beta` 0 and 1) and two team sizes (16, 64), blackboard channel. Measures: welfare per agent-second in each episode (the learning curve across episodes), the gap to the best fixed team exploration level (fixed-level arms on the same worlds), exploration share per agent, whether agents split into persistent scouts and foragers (spread of exploration level across agents, stable across episodes), and the free-riding index. Code: an episode loop that resets the world but not the members, and a `reset(keep=...)` on members that clears map-level state; the Phase 10 counterpart is the notes file in part 6 (b).
- **Hypothesis tests (fixed before running):** (1) time to get within a set distance of the ideal free distribution after patches move: blackboard faster than no communication (Phase 8 spec: tie-invariant distance, restricted mean time censored at the next move); (2) belief diversity: lower with the blackboard; (3) welfare loss with one liar (Phase 9): larger with the blackboard than with messages.
- **Secondary metrics:** time to find a moved patch, overlap of exploration between agents, how fast information spreads, communication cost per unit of reward, and a **free-riding index** (correlation between an agent's share of the team harvest and its share of patch discoveries; intended to turn strongly negative when some agents live off others' finds, but Phase 9 found it rising with free-riders instead, so as defined it does not flag free-riding in this world).
- **Belief diversity and herding (Lorenz et al. 2011):** the spread of agents' estimates of patch values (`banditAgent` value estimates; for the circuit agent, the spread of goal headings); and herding, meaning the team's shared estimate is confident (low spread) but wrong (far from the true values).
- **Statistics:** 30 seeds, paired comparisons on the same seeds, 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, effect sizes, scaling-curve fits. Many comparisons are made, so p-values get a Holm correction; only the three hypothesis tests and the primary metric count as confirmatory, everything else is exploratory. The Phase 8 spec (B6) makes the primary metric, (1) and (2) one Holm family of three, each tested once on 30 unseen seeds with a sign-flip permutation test; (3) needs Phase 9's liars and is its own family there.
- **Run time:** quick grid under 30 s (normal tests); full main grid under 10 min on a laptop (`#| slow`); side sweeps may be longer and are run on demand. Measured in Phase 8 (8 processes, `banditAgent` unchanged): quick grid about 4 s, main grid (1 680 runs) 280 s, side sweeps and study B 690 s (long cells need `nbdev-test --cell-timeout 0`).

## Phase 9: safety and mechanism design

Full spec: [phase9/](phase9/README.md). **Status: implemented (2026-10-11).** Run guide: [phase9/RUN.md](phase9/RUN.md). **Result:** H3 is supported on 30 unseen seeds, run once against its frozen pre-registration: one liar costs honest agents a larger share of their welfare on the blackboard than through messages (+0.041 [0.033, 0.049], dz 1.67, p = 0.0001; 4-7% vs about 1%). This completes the plan's hypothesis: the shared map makes teams faster and of one mind, and more vulnerable to liars. Exploratory: liars do almost all the damage (25% liars cost 24-50% of honest welfare on the blackboard); free-riders and saboteurs mostly cost team, not honest, welfare; private trust is the only defence that pays (at 64 agents it helps with and without liars); spot-check monitoring costs far more than it catches, and shared reputation and sanctions, though they revoke liars without ever sanctioning honest agents, do not raise honest welfare; detection from agents' own beliefs is perfect (AUC 1.00), while black-box reputation is fooled through messages (liars end up better rated); Phase 8's free-riding index does not flag free-riders as defined. The strike threshold was raised from the drafted 0.5-1.5 to 2.5 in calibration, before freezing, because honest noise exceeds 1.5. Decisions C1-C13 define the three bad-agent types as honest learners with one deviation each, four defences (none, private trust, shared reputation, graduated sanctions) with spot-check monitoring whose agent-seconds are counted, white-box (beliefs vs reports) and black-box detection AUC, and H3's cells: fixed compute, no defence, 8, 16 and 32 agents, one liar vs clean, relative loss of honest agents' welfare, seeds 300-329. A pilot on seeds 1000-1011 found that liars do real damage on the blackboard (25% liars: 24-46% of honest welfare) and almost none through messages, that one liar's contrast is large enough for H3 (dz about 1.85 on 12 seeds), that free-riders and saboteurs barely lower honest welfare per agent (so team welfare is reported too), and that private trust helps under attack but has a cost without one. Out of scope: defences for the hierarchical lead (it is attacked but not defended), bad circuit agents and their belief readout (Phase 8 found channels do not help circuit teams), and adaptive or colluding adversaries.

- **Bad agents:** a *liar* (reports high values for empty or danger locations and low values for good patches); a *free-rider* (only goes where others report value, never explores or writes); a *saboteur* (crowds the best reported patch). Swept from 0 to 1 agent, then 10% and 25% of the team.
- **Defences:** trust weighting (an agent trusts others as much as their reports matched its own experience), spot-checking claims, reputation.
- **Commons governance (Ostrom 1990):** monitoring (a share of reports is checked against what the checker experiences in that patch) plus **graduated sanctions** (a warning first, then lower write weight, then loss of write access). Compared with trust weighting at the same monitoring cost.
- **Interpretability:** numpy agents' beliefs can be read from their state (`banditAgent` value estimates; on the ring, the circuit agent's goal heading from its neural weights). Compare what an agent *believes* with what it *reports* to flag liars, and report detection AUC.
- **Outputs:** welfare vs number of bad agents for each channel and defence. Sanity check: with no bad agents, results match Phase 8.
- **Confirmatory test H3 (pre-registered in Phase 8, decision B6):** welfare loss with one liar is larger with the blackboard than with messages; its own Holm family, with its cells fixed in the Phase 9 spec before running, on unseen seeds, reusing `bench_grid.confirm`.

## Phase 10: language-model agents (Claude API)

Full spec: [phase10/](phase10/README.md). **Status: implemented (2026-10-12).** Results (exploratory, 47 988 calls, $33.50 with the pilot): on the task board every channel helps more as teams grow (no channel: welfare per agent-turn 0.99 alone, 0.35 at N = 8 with 60% duplicate work; claim blackboard: 0.94-0.99 at every size), and the numpy model ranks those cells as the LLM teams do (Spearman 0.75 [0.55, 0.93]); in text foraging nothing helps reliably, the blackboard reduces exploration and belief diversity at N = 8 (herding, as H2) and costs welfare, and numpy does not predict the LLM cells (0.02); a channel helped Claude Sonnet 5.5 less than Claude Haiku 5.5 at N = 4, but Sonnet at low effort also made many more mistakes, so the size contrast is unclear; framing and persistent notes did nothing clear, notes-only memory hurt. The pilot found no puzzle difficulty in the planned 30-70% band (Haiku 5.5 solves or runs out of tokens): difficulty 8 with `max_tokens` 8000, and 5 s stays in text foraging (user decisions, 2026-10-12). Decisions L1-L19: Claude Haiku 5.5 (`claude-haiku-5-5`) for every grid and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (`claude-sonnet-5-5`) for the reduced grid, adaptive thinking at low effort; a spending cap of $150 (confirmed by the user) against an estimate of about $50-70; an on-disk response cache as the only source of exact reruns, because these models reject temperature settings; stateless single-message turns with structured-output actions; refusal fallbacks off so a refused turn is never answered by another model; a pilot that sets the task-board difficulty and per-turn settings before the analysis plan is committed. Normal tests use a fake client and need neither the `anthropic` package nor network access.

**Why this phase matters for the role.** Its three representative projects are: (a) RL environments to train groups of Claudes, (b) agent affordances that unlock new capabilities, with the Bitter Lesson in mind, (c) a novel eval of how large teams of agents interact. As first planned (prompted agents on text foraging), Phase 10 was strong for (c), medium for (b) and weak for (a), and open to the objection that foraging is a toy task. The six parts below address those gaps without leaving the API-only budget.

**1. Two text tasks with the same structure.**
- **Text foraging:** the patch graph described in words. Each turn an agent sees its location, its last reward, the travel time to each location it can reach and the channel contents (board or messages), and replies with an action ("stay" or "go to location j", by name) and optionally a report (a location and a value; the Phase 10 spec allows no free-text messages). Locations are named, not given as grid coordinates, so the benchmark measures coordination and not LLM spatial reasoning (a known weakness that would confound the result). Used to compare with the numpy agents.
- **Task board (agentic version):** the same structure with real work. Each "patch" is a subtask with a checkable answer, generated from a seed (for example small logic or arithmetic puzzles, or finding the input that makes a short function return true, checked by running it), so tasks are unlimited and cannot be memorized. The board always holds more subtasks than agents. The mapping:

| Foraging | Task board |
|---|---|
| Harvest at a patch | Solve a subtask (checked automatically) |
| Crowding splits the harvest | Only the first correct answer scores; duplicates are wasted work |
| Travel time | Switching cost (turns spent reading a new subtask) |
| Patches move | Subtasks appear and expire (changing priorities) |
| Danger zone | Penalty for a wrong submission |
| Liar | Posts false "done" (solved) and "working" claims to the board |
| Ideal free distribution / social optimum | Best assignment of agents to subtasks from their values and solve rates (solve rates measured in the single-agent pilot; computed numerically; an upper bound) |

The Phase 8 and 9 metrics carry over where they apply: welfare, discoveries and liar robustness on both tasks; distance from the ideal, recovery time, belief diversity and herding on text foraging (on the task board every subtask's value is shown, so there is nothing to estimate). This targets (c) and answers the toy-task objection.

**2. Two model sizes (Bitter Lesson test, for (b)).** Run the smallest and a mid-size Claude model. Question: does each harness's benefit shrink as the model gets stronger? An affordance whose benefit shrinks is a stopgap; one whose benefit holds or grows is worth building. The mid-size model runs a reduced grid; with 3 seeds this is a directional result with wide confidence intervals, compared with the small model on the same cells and seeds.

**3. Tokens as compute.** Plot team performance against total tokens (agents x tokens per agent), with a fixed token limit per turn and equal budgets across team sizes (in the Phase 10 spec: equal agent-turns per team with tokens per turn capped, so total tokens are roughly equal; actual tokens recorded). This is the "groups of agents as test-time compute" axis in LLM units.

**4. Ready to train on (partly covers (a)).** Package both tasks with the Phase 5 interface (per-agent observations, one agent acting at a time), checkable rewards, individual / team / difference reward options, and JSONL trajectory logs. This shows the environment was designed for RL; it does not prove that training works. A real training demo (RL fine-tuning of a small open model, for example GRPO with TRL) needs a GPU and is listed as a later option, not part of this plan.

**5. Numpy covers large teams.** LLM runs stay at team sizes 1, 2, 4, 8. **Key finding:** does the numpy model predict how LLM teams do? Compare the two over all (channel, team size) cells, 16 per task, with Spearman correlation and a bootstrap confidence interval over seeds. Four channels alone would be too few points for a meaningful correlation. If the correlation is high, the numpy curves up to 64 agents become a credible prediction for large LLM teams, and harness ideas can be screened cheaply before LLM runs.

**6. Incentive framing and persistent memory (prompt-only, no training).** Two cheap tests of the Phase 8 questions with frozen models. (a) *Framing:* the same agents are told they are scored on their own result, on the team's, or on their own plus discovery credit; measure exploration share per agent, the free-riding index and team welfare. This is the LLM version of the group-vs-individual comparison and needs no weight updates. (b) *Persistent harness:* agents keep a notes file across episodes (lessons, not patch locations, which change), against a fresh start; measure welfare in later episodes on new world seeds (the LLM arm of the Phase 8 persistence side study). Both reuse the Phase 7 channels and memory settings, so they add no new environment code.

**Running it:**
- **Agents:** Claude models through the Anthropic API, with the Phase 7 channels mapped one to one. Memory settings become context management: last K turns, full history, or a running summary the agent writes for itself.
- **Reproducibility:** an on-disk response cache keyed by the full request. The current small and mid-size models reject sampling settings (temperature), so the cache, not the temperature, is what makes reruns exact (and free); live reruns are not expected to match.
- **Budget:** a hard spending cap in config; the cost is estimated before each run and recorded in `metrics.json`. The API key comes from the environment, the SDK's credential profile or the git-ignored `.env.local`, and is never committed, logged or cached; the cap is confirmed by you before any live request.
- **Scale:** small model: 4 channels x team sizes {1, 2, 4, 8} x 5 seeds x about 50 turns per agent, on both tasks (about 30 000 calls). Mid-size model: task board only, team sizes {1, 4}, 3 seeds (about 3 000 calls). Plus the pilot (about 1 100 calls) and the small model's arms (equal budget, framing, memory, persistence, liar: about 14 400 calls), about 49 000 calls in all; estimated about $36 at current prices. Ran: 47 988 calls for the grids plus about 1 960 in the pilot, $33.50 in all.

## Phase 11: a distributed-information task board

Full spec: [phase11/](phase11/README.md). **Status: implemented and run (2026-10-12)**: settings fixed by the numpy sweep (2 clues, answers below 30); live main, framing and liar runs $3.56; results in the Phase 11 README and section 7 of the [REPORT](../../results/multiagent/REPORT.md). The Phase 10 board turned out easy (Claude Haiku 5.5 solves almost every puzzle it opens), so it measured duplicate work, not division of hard labour. In Phase 11 each subtask's answer is fixed by modular clues ("x mod 3 = 2", ...) dealt to different agents, so no agent can solve it alone and communication is load-bearing; sharing costs a turn, which creates the incentive problem (under individual reward, sharing lets a teammate score). Rule agents check the board's dynamic range offline and fix its settings with a frozen rule; then Claude Haiku 5.5 runs (4 channels x N 2, 4, 8, a framing arm, a false-clue liar; $3.56; exploratory with a frozen plan). The board is packaged for RL (individual, team and credit rewards; batched episodes; trajectory exports; a teacher policy). RL training itself (GRPO on a small open model, as a standalone Colab notebook) is the next phase: [phase12/](phase12/README.md).

## Risks

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| The team environment is too simple to show interesting coordination (harnesses look alike, or teams saturate) | 2-D torus layout and difficulty settings; the Phase 6 prototype (task T1) must pass the dynamic-range check before any Phase 6 package code is written, and the demo re-checks it on the real world |
| The paper's circuit agent cannot work in 2-D (it encodes a direction, not a place) | Keep it on the ring; generic learners carry the 2-D experiments; a place-map model stays out of scope |
| Under fixed compute, larger teams see less world time | Report fixed-world-time curves next to fixed-compute curves |
| Too many comparisons produce false positives | Main grid and confirmatory tests fixed before running; Holm correction; side sweeps marked exploratory |
| Scope creep across six phases | Phases 5-8 are the core. Phases 9 and 10 can be cut down without losing the headline result |
| LLM costs and non-determinism | Hard cap, response cache, small grid; reduced grid for the mid-size model |
| Foraging results are dismissed as a toy task | The task board has the same structure with real, checkable work; all metrics carry over |
| Task-board subtasks are too easy or too hard for the models | Pilot first (Q7) |
| The numpy-vs-LLM comparison has too few points to mean anything | Compare over all (channel, team size) cells with a bootstrap interval, not over four channels |
| No evidence that the environment can train language models | Stated plainly; the environment is packaged ready for RL; a small fine-tuning demo is a later option |
| Phase 5 refactor breaks existing demos | Byte-identical `metrics.json` is the acceptance test |
| Ecology ideas become hand-coded agent rules, or are over-claimed for LLM agents | Use them only as references, metrics, harness settings and defences; label any rule-based agent (such as the hierarchical lead) as a baseline; report them as hypotheses tested |
